Tracing the hidden paths of microplastics through an urban estuary.

A two-year field study combining surface sampling, tide models, and spectral imaging to understand where plastic fragments accumulate—and when they return to the open coast.

01

The question

When the tide turns, where does the plastic go?

Most surveys tell us how much plastic is present. This project asks how it moves—linking particle type, shoreline geometry, stormwater discharge, and tidal energy across one connected system.

Researchers sampled the same 18 stations at low, rising, and high tide. Every fragment was photographed, measured, and classified with Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy. A transport model then connected those observations to hourly current direction.

02

What we found

Three signals changed our working model.

01 / RETENTION

62%

of observed fragments remained inside the marsh edge after a full tide cycle.

Vegetated inlets behaved as temporary reservoirs, especially for fibers shorter than 2 mm.

02 / TIMING

2.4×

higher particle flux during the first post-rain ebb tide.

The strongest movement occurred 4–7 hours after peak stormwater discharge.

03 / MATERIAL

47%

of classified particles were synthetic textile fibers.

Polyester and nylon dominated at the upper-estuary stations closest to dense housing.
